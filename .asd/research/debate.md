# Stage 2 Debate: PlayStation Digital Continuity, Competition and Preservation

**As of:** July 16, 2026  
**Scope:** New PlayStation console releases from January 2028; United States, European Union and United Kingdom. This is policy and business analysis, not legal advice.  
**Proposal under debate:** Stage 1's prospective title-level continuity standard and escrow, preceded by licence/lifecycle disclosures and restored retail sale of title-specific codes, plus a limited optional transferability pilot.

## Proposal summary

Stage 1 rejects both the digital-only status quo and a universal platform-end-of-life unlock. It instead asks SIE to make every newly certified or recontracted title declare a title/service-specific end-of-life (EOL) plan. Depending on the game's architecture and rights, the plan would provide one or more of: an offline patch/build; a local or private-server package or documented protocol; continued redownload and offline entitlement validation for a stated period; or an encrypted archival deposit when customer release is infeasible. It adds advance shutdown notice, data export where applicable, and a pro-rata refund or store credit when actual life is shorter than a prominently disclosed minimum. Entitlements should migrate to replacement compatible hardware rather than remain bound to one console.

Alongside continuity, the proposal would require a pre-purchase rights label and reopen wholesale distribution of title-specific PlayStation codes so retailers can compete on price. A small, publisher-opt-in pilot would test a transferable licence with one active entitlement at a time. Stage 1 does not endorse a general digital-resale right or fixed discount for weaker rights.

The proposal tries to connect three real but distinct problems:

- **Digital ownership:** a full-price purchase is presently an account-bound, generally revocable, non-transferable licence, not title ownership; account, entitlement and compatible-hardware dependencies remain.
- **Fair pricing:** after discs disappear, SIE's store could be the only title-level digital channel unless retail codes return. Price fairness is therefore more credibly a competition, disclosure and lost-capabilities question than a simple manufacturing-cost calculation.
- **Preservation:** customer playability, institutional preservation and public scholarly access are separate outcomes. An offline consumer build does not preserve source/build context, while an encrypted archive deposit does not ensure customer access.

This connected framing is sound. The package is not yet an implementable standard. It leaves undefined which actor owes which outcome, what minimum outcome every purchased game must satisfy, how conformity is tested, how archives may lawfully use deposits, who pays, and who adjudicates trigger disputes.

### Evidence and citation audit

- **Verified:** SIE's July 1, 2026 announcement says physical game-disc production for *all new games releasing on PlayStation consoles* ends starting January 2028, with new releases available on PlayStation Store and “at retailers in digital formats only”; pre-cutoff releases are unaffected.[1] The scope ambiguities identified by Stage 1 remain real.
- **Verified:** SIE separately announced staged PS3 purchasing closures and global PS3/Vita purchasing closure in July 2027, while promising redownload only “for the foreseeable future.”[2]
- **Verified:** Current US terms call a store transaction a personal licence, say the customer does not own the product, and describe content licences as revocable and normally non-transferable. UK/EEA terms similarly state that the customer buys a personal, normally non-transferable licence and may lose paid products after account suspension or closure. The software EULA says offline modes after online-service closure are not guaranteed.[3][4][5]
- **Verified with a retrieval caveat:** Sony's FY2025 supplemental report is indexed on Sony's domain and reports a 78% full-year digital-download ratio and 85% in Q4 for PS4/PS5 full-game unit sales.[6] Direct automated retrieval returned HTTP 403, but the official document metadata and figures were independently indexed. The metric still does not establish why customers chose digital, the share of people dependent on discs, or willingness to trade rights for price.
- **Verified:** California AB 2426 regulates use of “buy” and “purchase” for revocable digital goods, requiring a distinct licence disclosure or acknowledgment; it creates no durability, transfer or preservation right.[7]
- **Verified:** The 2024 US DMCA rule permits limited circumvention for local personal play after server support ceases and for eligible institutions under specified conditions. Institutional access remains on premises; the Copyright Office rejected broader off-premises access because proponents had not shown likely non-infringing use and because of market-risk concerns.[8] Stage 1's short summary was directionally correct but understated how narrow archive access remains.
- **Verified, but Stage 1 overstates one point:** the European Commission declined, at this stage, to propose a keep-playable legal obligation. It will start an exchange by the end of 2026 aimed at drawing up an industry EOL code of conduct and will report on application of Directive 2019/770.[9] Stage 1's phrase “the EU's planned code” is supportable if understood as an intended drafting process, not an adopted code. Its claim that the Commission rejected a mandate “as disproportionate” is not established by the cited short press release; the full Communication, not the cited page alone, is needed for that characterization. No code text, metrics or enforcement commitment exists yet.
- **Verified:** Directive 2019/770 supplies conformity, update and remedy rules but sets no specific duration and does not require continued use after commercial supply ends; the Commission expressly confirmed that limitation.[9][10] National transposition and facts still determine individual remedies.
- **Verified:** the UK government said there is no requirement to support old software and no current plan to amend the law, while emphasizing the Consumer Rights Act and clear marketing. Since April 2025 the CMA has direct consumer-enforcement powers, but no game-EOL rule or relevant concluded enforcement case was identified.[11][12]
- **Verified with a scope warning:** VGHF's 2023 study sampled 1,500 US games released before 2010 and estimated 13% commercial availability (+/-2.5%, 95% confidence).[13] It measures commercial availability, not physical survival, technical completeness, rights clearance or demand. Stage 1 handled this distinction correctly.
- **Verified:** SIE ended broad retailer sales of full-game download codes in 2019.[14] A pending US antitrust class settlement concerns allegations that ending game-specific vouchers raised prices for qualifying titles. SIE denies wrongdoing, the settlement is only preliminarily approved, and a fairness hearing is scheduled for October 15, 2026; it is evidence of a live competition concern, not a judicial finding that retail-code removal was unlawful or that restoring codes will reduce every price.[15]
- **Not independently verified:** Stage 1 provides no evidence for actual per-title escrow cost, percentage of titles technically convertible, private-server security incidence, consumer willingness to pay for transferability, expected resale cannibalization, entitlement-service failure rates, or the claim that this package would be a net “trust differentiator.” These are material assumptions, not minor data gaps.

## Stakeholder positions

### Players and consumer advocates

**Best case for the proposal:** Ending discs removes a transferable authentication/data carrier, offline fallback for some titles, lending/resale and a competing retail channel. Disclosures alone merely explain dependence. A prospective continuity obligation converts vague expectations into testable product attributes, and retailer codes can restore some launch and sale-price competition without requiring a new copyright doctrine. Refunds give disclosed minimum terms economic force.

**Likely objection:** A menu lets publishers choose escrow instead of customer playability and then market every game as “continuity planned.” Store credit keeps an injured customer inside SIE's ecosystem and can be worthless near platform failure. “Where feasible,” “where safe,” and “where practicable” can swallow every obligation. A transfer pilot available only on low-risk, old or unpopular games may generate a predetermined finding of weak demand. Advocates will demand a non-waivable baseline: purchased self-contained modes must remain playable offline, account discipline must not destroy lawful local use, and unavoidable shutdown before the disclosed term should trigger cash-equivalent remedies.

**Acceptable compromise:** Separate a mandatory consumer baseline from optional preservation outcomes; use plain labels with a calendar date or objective event; allow cash or original-payment refunds where law requires or the platform caused the failure; protect local lawful play after account closure while preserving proportionate sanctions for online services.

### SIE and the PlayStation platform

**Best case for the proposal:** SIE can use certification leverage to require plans before rights and staff disappear. Standard APIs for offline receipts, dependency manifests and deposits could be cheaper and safer than emergency catalog remediation. Retail codes answer the strongest competition criticism while all activation still runs through SIE. A credible standard could reduce regulatory exposure and preserve trust before the disc cutoff.

**Likely objection:** SIE would become guarantor for third-party conduct it does not fully control. Migratable offline receipts weaken revocation, account recovery and anti-fraud controls. Private servers or protocol documentation can expose attack surfaces, copyrighted platform interfaces, anti-cheat logic and signing assumptions. Long-tail redownload and entitlement promises create indefinite operational liabilities across hardware transitions. Wholesale codes create fraud, chargeback, regional arbitrage and channel-conflict costs, and can reduce PlayStation Store margin. Certification delays may drive publishers toward competing platforms.

**Acceptable compromise:** SIE supplies and funds common platform tooling, limits its guarantee to platform-controlled dependencies, requires publisher warranties and indemnities for publisher-controlled deposits, uses audited escrow rather than broad public release, and permits emergency security exceptions subject to independent review and remedies. Retail competition could begin with controlled authorized resellers, non-discriminatory access and auditable wholesale terms rather than unrestricted bearer codes.

### AAA publishers and studios

**Best case for the proposal:** Prospective clearance is materially better than retroactive mandates. A title-type menu recognizes that single-player, peer-to-peer, dedicated-server and persistent-world games are different. Predictable EOL rules can reduce reputational crises and permit planned decommissioning. Advance architecture work may also reduce ongoing server cost.

**Likely objection:** A “final build” is a new production milestone with QA, accessibility, ratings, localization, privacy, middleware and security work after revenue has declined. Music, brands, talent, middleware, user-generated content and cloud services may not be licensed for consumer server distribution or archival access. Depositing server code can expose trade secrets shared across active titles. A private server can facilitate cheating against still-active versions or create consumer confusion about moderation and safety. Pro-rata refunds can turn every shutdown into an uncapped contingent liability and may discourage ambitious online games.

**Acceptable compromise:** Apply prospectively to contracts signed after a realistic lead time; classify modes separately; allow verified clean-room or functionally limited continuity packages; cap remedies against a clearly disclosed service term without making that term a safe harbor for deception; protect shared engines by depositing reproducible binaries, interfaces and title-specific components where source cannot be separated.

### Indie publishers and studios

**Best case for the proposal:** SIE-provided templates, automated tests and subsidized escrow could make continuity easier than bespoke shutdown work. A rights label can distinguish an offline-friendly indie game from a more dependent AAA product. Insolvency-triggered deposits are especially valuable when a small studio disappears.

**Likely objection:** Fixed certification and legal costs are regressive. An indie may rely on inexpensive third-party backends whose terms prohibit deposit and may lack staff to maintain an EOL branch. Mandatory insurance, audits or annual deposits can make PlayStation release uneconomic. A broad exemption for small firms, however, creates the worst consumer risk exactly where failure is more likely and invites publishers to restructure around thresholds.

**Acceptable compromise:** SIE bears common tooling and base escrow fees, requirements scale by dependencies and revenue rather than studio label, deposit updates are automated from release pipelines, and genuine impossibility leads to prominent term/risks disclosure plus a funded remedy rather than silent exemption.

### Retailers and competing storefronts

**Best case for the proposal:** Title-specific codes permit retailers to discount, bundle, accept cash, offer gifts and serve customers without payment cards. The pending *Caccuri* settlement shows that loss of this channel is not a theoretical competition concern, though it proves no violation.[15] Retail presence also supports console discovery and hardware trade-ins.

**Likely objection:** Codes alone do not create competition if SIE controls wholesale availability, activation, territorial rules, refund processing, discount windows and margin. Retailers may become low-margin fraud and customer-support intermediaries while SIE retains customer data and post-sale monetization. Wallet cards are not title competition and can strand balances.

**Acceptable compromise:** Transparent, non-discriminatory reseller eligibility; title access and wholesale terms that allow independent discounts; clear allocation of refunds/chargebacks; no parity clause preventing lower retailer prices; auditable reporting. The proposal should say whether publishers may issue codes directly or only through SIE.

### US regulators

**Position:** Disclosure is the clearest near-term route. California already requires licence clarity, while US copyright and first-sale doctrine do not create a general digital-game transfer right. Competition agencies or private plaintiffs may scrutinize exclusive title-level distribution, but *Caccuri* is unresolved and console market definition, business justification and consumer harm remain contested. A voluntary contractual transfer pilot is legally safer than claiming statutory first sale, but it still requires publisher authorization. Preservation exemptions do not compel SIE to supply keys or code and do not authorize broad public access.[7][8][15]

**Likely regulatory compromise:** Enforce truthful labels and announced terms; examine retail exclusion with evidence; support narrowly defined archival agreements; avoid prescribing one architecture. Federal action is uncertain, state-by-state disclosure rules risk fragmentation, and arbitration/class waivers can weaken private enforcement.

### EU regulators

**Position:** EU law offers stronger conformity and unfair-terms tools but no perpetual-playability rule. The Commission has explicitly declined a new obligation for now and instead intends an industry-code process.[9] A measurable continuity label, notice, remedies and archive partnership fit that process better than an absolute private-server mandate. However, a purely voluntary code can entrench the industry's preferred low baseline and fragment implementation across Member States.

**Likely regulatory compromise:** A code defining title classes, minimum information, notice and remedies; monitoring under Directive 2019/770 and national law; later legislative review if compliance is poor. Transferability remains legally uncertain for composite video games: *UsedSoft* is not a safe basis for a general game-resale right, while *Tom Kabinet* cuts against broad exhaustion for downloaded protected content.[16][17]

### UK regulators

**Position:** Current policy rejects a new old-software support duty but preserves satisfactory-quality, description and durability rules and stronger direct CMA enforcement.[11][12] The proposal's disclosure and term-remedy components fit current policy; mandatory offline builds and archive release go beyond it. The government can plausibly wait for evidence from the EU code and the 2028 transition.

**Likely regulatory compromise:** Require material EOL information to be prominent, police false durability claims and unfair revocation, and encourage an industry standard. Without game-specific guidance, individual consumer remedies remain uncertain and costly to pursue.

### Archives, libraries and museums

**Best case for the proposal:** Contractual deposit can preserve materials that legal circumvention exemptions cannot create. Reproducible builds, server components, metadata, keys and rights records deposited during production would be a large improvement over acquiring an encrypted retail package after shutdown.

**Likely objection:** “Accredited archive/escrow agent” is undefined. Escrow firms protect a beneficiary's business continuity; cultural institutions preserve, describe and provide access. Those are not interchangeable roles. A release trigger without an express licence to decrypt, migrate, emulate, copy, disclose to researchers and retain personal-data-safe records may yield a legally unusable vault. In the US, even eligible institutions remain constrained to on-premises access under the current DMCA exemption.[8] Archives may also be unable to meet platform-grade key security or recurring audit costs.

**Acceptable compromise:** Separate commercial escrow custody from eventual archival accession; name eligible institution criteria; include an irrevocable preservation licence, permitted transformations, access conditions, privacy scrubbing and successor arrangements; fund storage, migration and security for decades; deposit documentation and build environments, not merely executables.

## Strongest arguments for

1. **The intervention occurs at the only practical time.** Certification and publishing contracts are when SIE has leverage, the build works, staff are available and third-party rights can include EOL uses. Waiting until shutdown predictably produces missing code, expired licences and no budget.

2. **Title-level triggers match actual failure.** Required-server shutdown or entitlement/redownload cessation can kill a game long before the console platform closes. An objective title/service trigger is superior to “platform EOL,” which SIE could delay indefinitely.

3. **The menu avoids a technically false universal promise.** Self-contained modes can plausibly receive an offline entitlement/build; persistent worlds often cannot be faithfully reduced to an executable. Allowing different outcomes is more credible than requiring every game to become an offline ISO.

4. **Disclosure, competition and continuity reinforce one another.** Labels make durability and dependency visible; retailer codes test whether a single storefront is inflating net prices; continuity protects value when the seller exits. Any one alone is incomplete: cheap codes do not preserve games, escrow does not discipline launch prices, and labels do not keep purchases usable.

5. **Retail codes target the strongest fair-pricing mechanism.** The proposal avoids the weak claim that digital price must equal physical price minus manufacturing. Competing sellers can reveal actual price competition while publishers retain control of list price and supply. The *Caccuri* allegations make empirical monitoring especially warranted, though they are not proven findings.[15]

6. **Escrow addresses insolvency and abandonment without immediate disclosure.** Properly verified deposits can keep secrets protected during commercial life while preventing total loss when a studio or service disappears. It is a known software-continuity mechanism, even if applying it catalog-wide is untested.

7. **The approach is more legally modest than digital first sale.** Contractually granting offline or transfer rights does not depend on courts extending exhaustion. A pilot can test fraud, demand and cannibalization without asserting a universal legal entitlement.

8. **It creates auditable commitments.** A dated support term, dependency classification, tested final package, notice clock and remedy are more enforceable than “foreseeable future.” They can support regulators, customers, SIE and archives with the same lifecycle record.

## Strongest arguments against

1. **There is no defined minimum, so the “standard” can certify destruction.** A publisher may select archival escrow because consumer release is “infeasible,” even where an offline mode could have been designed. If every menu choice passes, the scheme guarantees paperwork, not continuity. Escrow is not a substitute for the buyer's use right.

2. **The duty allocation is unresolved.** SIE sells many digital products and controls entitlements; publishers control design and rights; studios control code; archives control custody. The proposal does not specify who is legally responsible to the customer, who cures a failed deposit, who pays refunds, or whose obligation survives insolvency. Indemnities between firms do not answer the consumer-facing question.

3. **Technical feasibility is asserted, not demonstrated at PlayStation scale.** No catalog audit supports “medium” feasibility, no prototype shows migratable offline receipts surviving replacement hardware without an online authority, and no threat model covers key extraction, rollback, cloned consoles, entitlement resale or compromised firmware. The proposal could create a durable piracy primitive or still fail when secure hardware and signing chains age out.

4. **Escrow can become preservation theater.** Deposits drift, builds depend on unavailable compilers/services, shared server code cannot be released, encryption keys are lost, and the beneficiary lacks a use licence. Verification of every major release adds recurring expense. A binary that boots is not necessarily a faithful service, and an archive that possesses code may remain legally unable to offer meaningful access.

5. **Private servers change the product and transfer risk.** Empty populations, absent moderation, unavailable payments/cloud assets, personal data, cheating and unsafe user-generated content can make a private server misleading or dangerous. Releasing protocols can expose vulnerabilities in related live titles. “Where safe” is necessary but creates an easy opt-out unless an independent reviewer decides.

6. **A minimum support term can become a scheduled death date.** Firms may choose the shortest marketable term, price contingent refunds into launch economics, stop improving continuity after the floor, or design more modes as subscriptions to escape purchase rules. A pro-rata formula also makes late-life breach cheap even where total loss matters most to preservation.

7. **Retail-code competition may be nominal.** If SIE sets wholesale price near retail, limits inventory, synchronizes promotions or imposes territorial restrictions, retailers cannot discipline price. Bearer codes also increase fraud and arbitrage. The proposal needs market-access rules, not merely technical issuance.

8. **Transferability is a costly distraction.** Perfect digital copies do not degrade; one-active-copy enforcement requires continuing platform authority, undermining independence at EOL. Account theft, chargebacks, DLC, saves, trophies, family access, sanctions and regional SKUs complicate title transfer. Publisher opt-in and title selection can bias the pilot, while consumers may infer ownership rights that the rest of the licence denies.

9. **Compliance burdens can reduce games, modes or experimentation.** Publishers may skip PlayStation, remove online modes, use shorter terms, increase prices or classify products as subscriptions. Small studios face proportionally higher legal and QA costs. Consumers can lose choice even while each remaining title is more durable.

10. **The legal route is weaker than Stage 1's confidence suggests.** The EU has not adopted a code or mandate; the UK has declined a new duty; US exemptions are narrow; and exhaustion does not safely authorize game resale. A global standard therefore depends primarily on SIE voluntarily changing contracts and architecture, not on imminent harmonized regulation.

11. **Refunds do not preserve culture or restore network value.** A pro-rata credit can compensate part of a transaction but cannot recreate communities, user histories, evolving worlds or historically authentic server states. Conversely, preserving a server snapshot may retain personal data and harmful content that cannot lawfully be deposited unchanged.

12. **The package can cross-subsidize a minority outcome without evidence.** Sony reports digital uptake but not demand for offline migration, transfer or archival access. Costs could be spread across all buyers while benefits accrue to a smaller long-term-use population. That may still be justified as consumer infrastructure or cultural policy, but Stage 1 has not established the distributional case.

## Critical tradeoffs

| Tradeoff | Position favoring stronger continuity | Position favoring narrower obligations | Decision pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer use vs archival custody | Every purchase needs a usable post-trigger outcome | Escrow may be the only lawful outcome for complex titles | Make them separate tracks; do not let deposit satisfy a consumer guarantee unless impossibility is independently verified and disclosed before sale |
| Durability vs security/revocation | Offline receipts must survive account and service failure | Revocation is needed for fraud, refunds, sanctions and compromised accounts | Preserve lawful local play while allowing narrowly scoped online sanctions; define treatment of refunded/stolen entitlements and independent appeal |
| Faithful preservation vs feasible approximation | Preserve server code, builds, state and context | Full live worlds, populations and licensed services cannot be recreated | Label authenticity/completeness; preserve components and documentation even when public play is impossible |
| Long support vs innovation/cost | Product-like prices justify a meaningful floor | Long liabilities deter risky online designs and small entrants | Set mode-specific outcomes, not perpetual operation; provide common tooling and scaled funding |
| Notice/remedies vs premature EOL | Terms make expectations enforceable | A term becomes a shutdown option priced by pro-rata liability | Require a reasoned shutdown report and usage assessment; do not make the minimum term a liability safe harbor |
| Retail competition vs platform economics | Multiple title sellers replace lost disc competition | SIE funds security, commerce, CDN and support and seeks store margin | Require non-discriminatory wholesale access while allowing a transparent platform fee; assess actual net-price effects |
| Transferability vs primary sales | Restores a central physical-copy capability | Perfect substitutes and fraud can cannibalize sales | Keep pilot separate, randomized where possible, one title entitlement plus defined DLC rules, and sunset unless evidence supports expansion |
| Global consistency vs local law | One label and baseline reduce confusion | Remedies, exhaustion, privacy and archive rights vary | Use a global technical baseline with jurisdiction-specific legal text and remedies; avoid calling the product “owned” |
| Transparency vs cognitive overload | More attributes expose real differences | Consumers ignore dense labels and choose on price | Use a short standardized label linked to machine-readable detail; test comprehension, not merely display |
| Archive access vs market substitution | Preservation without access has little cultural value | Remote access may compete with reissues and leak protected assets | Contractually define controlled research access and revisit as law changes; current US on-premises limits remain |

### Principal failure modes and perverse incentives

- Publishers label required online functions as the core artistic work so no offline minimum applies.
- Games move from “purchase” to nominal subscriptions or free-to-play products to avoid duties while monetizing heavily through add-ons.
- SIE approves boilerplate escrow plans without build verification to meet a public compliance metric.
- Deposits contain final binaries but omit server state, build tools, keys, third-party dependencies or legal permissions.
- Minimum terms converge on the shortest period consumers tolerate, turning disclosure into permission to terminate.
- Retailers receive codes but no economically viable margin or independent discount authority.
- Transfer-pilot titles are selected to minimize resale appeal, making a negative result non-generalizable.
- Store credit replaces cash, retains platform revenue and understates consumer harm.
- Insolvent publishers leave SIE, archives or players with unfunded maintenance and privacy liabilities.
- Security exceptions become permanent secret exemptions with no external scrutiny.
- An escrow breach leaks signing material or reusable server technology before EOL.
- Archive “accreditation” favors well-funded institutions and excludes community experts who possess much of the practical preservation knowledge.

### Implementation and coordination blockers

- SIE must publish the disc policy's territorial, platform, publisher, reprint and retail-code scope before contracts can be aligned.
- A title taxonomy and dependency manifest must distinguish self-contained modes, optional online features, authentication-dependent modes, dedicated multiplayer and persistent services.
- Certification needs objective tests for offline cold-start, time rollback, account-service loss, replacement compatible hardware and reproducible deposits.
- Publisher agreements must secure EOL distribution, modification, server-operation, emulation, archive, research and successor rights from all relevant contributors.
- The parties need a data-protection process for saves, telemetry, user-generated content, moderation records and server databases, including deletion and export duties.
- SIE must define entitlement succession after death, account closure, platform or publisher insolvency, refunds, chargebacks and regional migration.
- An independent body must decide infeasibility, emergency exceptions, trigger occurrence and failed conformance; SIE cannot credibly be sole seller, certifier and appellate body.
- Funding must cover platform tooling, per-title engineering, deposit verification, escrow renewal, archival migration and eventual controlled access. No current budget or allocation formula exists.
- Retail-code rules need wholesale pricing, inventory, promotion, territorial, fraud, refund and customer-support terms.
- Cross-border implementation must map EU national transpositions, UK remedies, US state disclosure law, copyright/anti-circumvention, export controls and sanctions.

## Final judgment

**Judgment: retain the direction, but require major revision before adoption.** The factual case for intervention is credible: the disc cutoff is confirmed; digital purchases are licences with infrastructure dependencies; retail title-code competition previously existed and was removed; and commercial availability of older games is poor. Stage 1 is also right to reject a universal platform-EOL ISO and a full rights-tier matrix.

The major flaw is that Option 4 is a menu of desirable artifacts, not a standard that guarantees an outcome. It permits escrow to substitute for consumer continuity, leaves “infeasible/safe/practicable” to the controlled firms, and omits enforceable duty allocation, verification, funding and archive-use rights. Those omissions create a high risk of symbolic compliance and perverse design choices. The proposal should not be represented as the present “best balance” until these are resolved.

Revision should split the package into four independently governed modules:

1. **Consumer continuity floor:** for each purchased mode, disclose a dated minimum service period and post-trigger outcome. Require offline continuation for genuinely self-contained modes. For dependent modes, require a specified reduced-function outcome or original-payment/cash-equivalent remedy. Escrow alone does not satisfy this floor.
2. **Preservation deposit:** require a separately funded, periodically verified deposit with explicit preservation, migration and controlled-access licences, privacy treatment and successor custody. Deposit scope may vary by title, but omissions must be recorded.
3. **Retail competition regime:** restore title-level authorized resale channels only with non-discriminatory wholesale access, independent discounting and auditable price/outcome data. Measure whether net prices, availability and cash/gifting access improve.
4. **Transferability experiment:** keep this optional and separate from the 2028 transition. Pre-register title selection, technical rules and metrics; include DLC/refund/fraud handling; do not infer legal ownership or general market effects from a biased small sample.

An independent conformity and exceptions process is indispensable. Without it, SIE and publishers can define feasibility, certify themselves, set the term, trigger shutdown and choose store credit. With it, the revised model is a plausible prospective co-regulatory pilot, especially for the EU code process. It is not yet justified as a universal legal mandate, and disagreement remains over whether customer playability should override rights-holder control for persistent online works.

## Evidence still needed

- SIE's binding interpretation of “at retailers in digital formats only,” including whether title-specific codes return and whether retailers can set final prices.
- Contractual scope of the 2028 cutoff across third parties, territories, generations, cross-generation titles, reprints, imports and limited editions.
- A representative PlayStation catalog dependency audit by title and mode, with reproducible criteria and error rates.
- A prototype showing offline cold-start and entitlement migration to replacement compatible hardware after simulated PSN/redownload loss, plus an external threat model.
- Security evidence on key separation, rollback resistance, code/protocol release, shared engines, anti-cheat and vulnerability handling after vendor support ends.
- Per-title and platform-level cost estimates for final-build QA, rights clearance, deposits, verification, long-term storage, migration and dispute administration, separated for AAA and indie titles.
- Failure-rate evidence from real software escrows: deposit freshness, reproducible-build success, release disputes, security incidents and long-term costs. Generic escrow marketing is not enough.
- Executed-model contract clauses covering EOL modification/distribution, server operation, archival copying, emulation, research access, third-party middleware and insolvency.
- Named candidate archives and escrow agents with capacity, governance, key-security, privacy, funding and successor plans in each region.
- Consumer research measuring comprehension of the proposed label, expected meaning of “purchase,” acceptable support terms, preferred remedies and willingness to pay for offline/transfer rights.
- Distributional data on disc reliance, resale/lending, disability/accessibility needs, cash use, broadband constraints and storage costs by region and household type.
- Causal pricing evidence comparing matched physical/digital titles, sale frequency, taxes, retailer margins and the pre/post-2019 effects of PlayStation game-specific voucher removal. The pending settlement is not a merits finding.[15]
- Retailer evidence on viable wholesale margins, fraud/chargeback rates, inventory controls, customer support and independent discount authority.
- A pre-registered transfer pilot design with representative titles, control groups and metrics for new-sale cannibalization, fraud, price, liquidity, player welfare and publisher revenue.
- Jurisdiction-specific legal opinions on composite-game exhaustion, contractual transfers, anti-circumvention, archive release, privacy, unfair terms, refunds and enforceability against successors.
- The final EU EOL code text, governance, metrics and compliance consequences after the Commission's stakeholder process; Stage 1 cannot assume these outcomes.[9]
- Evidence defining when continued operation is genuinely unsafe or impossible and who can review confidential proof without publicly exposing vulnerabilities.
