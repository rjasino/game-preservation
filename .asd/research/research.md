# Stage 1 Research: PlayStation Disc Exit and Durable Digital Rights

**As of:** July 16, 2026  
**Scope:** PlayStation console games; United States, European Union and United Kingdom; ownership, pricing and preservation. This is policy/business analysis, not legal advice.

## Problem framing

### Decision and lifecycle

Sony Interactive Entertainment (SIE) has confirmed that it will discontinue physical-disc production for **all new games releasing on PlayStation consoles starting January 2028**. New games will instead be sold through PlayStation Store and “at retailers in digital formats only.” Games released, or due to release, on disc before the cutoff are expressly unaffected.[1] The decision therefore concerns new PlayStation releases, not the continued operation of existing discs, all Blu-ray production, or necessarily every future reprint of an older title. Sony has not publicly defined which console generations, territories, retail digital products, exceptions, or certification rules the policy covers.

The decision should be evaluated across a title's whole lifecycle: certification and launch; patches/DLC and active sale; delisting; end of online service; storefront closure; loss of redownload/authentication services; and eventual hardware failure. “Platform EOL” is not a sufficiently precise trigger: a game may lose servers or rights years before a console/store closes, while downloads may continue after purchasing ends. On July 1, 2026, Sony separately announced PS3 and PS Vita store purchasing closures (globally in July 2027 after earlier regional PS3 closures) but promised redownloads only “for the foreseeable future.”[2]

### Business model and rights chain

PlayStation is a closed console ecosystem. SIE controls hardware trust, system software, certification, account entitlements and the PlayStation Store. A third-party publisher generally controls the game and commercial rights; developers, music/likeness licensors, middleware and anti-cheat vendors, server/cloud providers, unions and local distributors may hold additional rights or impose continuing obligations. A disc is both a transferable physical copy and often an authentication/data carrier, but patches, DLC or server functions may still be essential. A download is an account-bound licence plus encrypted content and platform permission; local installation does not by itself guarantee future reinstall, activation or complete playability.

Sony's April 2026 US terms say buyers receive a personal, revocable, non-transferable licence and do not own the product.[3] UK/EEA terms similarly call it a personal licence, warn that account suspension/closure can remove paid content, and permit closure after 36 months' inactivity following notice where lawful.[4] Sony's software EULA says offline modes after online-service closure are not guaranteed.[5] The input's “Level 0: nothing; access is a server record” therefore overstates the present condition: supported consoles can keep locally installed games usable offline in some configurations, but entitlement recovery and reinstall remain infrastructure-dependent.

### Evidence classification

- **Confirmed facts:** Sony's January 2028 new-release disc cutoff and stated protection for pre-cutoff releases; the PS3/Vita purchasing closures; Sony's licence terms; and Sony's FY2025 full-game digital-download ratio of 78% across PS4/PS5 (85% in Q4), calculated by unit sales, not revenue or unique players.[1][2][6]
- **Unconfirmed/ambiguous:** Whether retailers will again sell title-specific codes (Sony ended its global full-game digital-at-retail program in 2019), whether gift cards count as “digital formats,” the treatment of limited runs/reprints, territorial rollout, and future hardware with optical drives.[1][7]
- **Assumptions in the input:** Publishers always preferred licensing; discs alone caused PS4's early sales advantage; AAA budgets are generally “unsustainable”; digital is inevitable; and a first mover would gain enough trust/revenue to offset cost. These are plausible narratives or forecasts, not established by the cited evidence.
- **Normative claims:** Product-like prices should buy durable use; price should track rights; and publishers owe an offline/preservation outcome. These are defensible policy positions, not present universal legal duties.
- **Forecasts:** Disc collecting may remain a niche; EU pressure may produce stronger rights; and digital-only distribution will increase platform power. Directionally plausible, but market and regulatory outcomes remain uncertain.

## Key findings

1. **The foundational claim is real but the headline can mislead.** This is an official SIE policy for new PlayStation-console games from January 2028, not a rumor and not an immediate destruction of existing disc libraries.[1] It is independent of Sony's February 2025 exit from blank recordable Blu-ray media.

2. **The market supports a digital shift, not the removal of all consumer safeguards.** Sony reports 78% of FY2025 PS4/PS5 full-game units were digital.[6] That metric excludes add-on content, does not reveal consumer preference among otherwise equal rights/prices, and leaves a material 22% physical segment. It does not measure accessibility constraints, lending/resale value, rural bandwidth, household data caps, preservation, or retailer competition.

3. **Digital price parity is not proof of overcharging.** Digital removes pressing, packaging, freight, inventory and some retail costs, and SIE can retain more margin on its own store. It also carries payment, bandwidth, storage, support, fraud, tax, platform and long-tail infrastructure costs; game development and marketing dominate many budgets. Prices reflect demand, channel strategy, tax and bargaining power, not cost-plus accounting. The stronger fair-pricing case is loss of resale/lending and storefront competition without a transparent rights or price response, not the unsupported claim that every digital copy should receive a fixed manufacturing-cost discount.

4. **Legal protection differs, but none of the three regions presently guarantees perpetual playability or digital resale.**

| Region | Current position and implication |
|---|---|
| US | Physical first sale protects disposition of a lawfully owned copy, but internet transfer normally makes a new copy; *ReDigi* rejected that route for digital music.[8] California AB 2426, effective 2025, requires clear licence disclosure when using “buy/purchase,” but does not create ownership or continued access.[9] DMCA section 1201 exemptions permit limited circumvention for discontinued-server games, including personal local play and qualified institutional preservation, but do not supply code, erase contract/copyright risk, or create general distribution rights.[10] |
| EU | Directive 2019/770 requires conformity, expected functionality/continuity, updates in relevant periods and remedies such as repair, price reduction or termination; it does not itself mandate perpetual operation.[11] *UsedSoft* recognizes exhaustion for some perpetual downloaded computer-program licences, but games are composite works and later *Tom Kabinet* confines digital exhaustion for downloaded content; assuming a general EU right to resell console games is unsafe.[12][13] On June 16, 2026, the Commission declined to propose a mandatory keep-playable law as disproportionate, instead committing to explore an industry EOL code by year-end and review discontinuation under the Directive.[14] |
| UK | Consumer Rights Act 2015 requires digital content to be satisfactory, as described and sufficiently durable, with repair/replacement and price-reduction remedies.[15] The government stated in 2025 that it had no plan to require support for old software, while retaining existing consumer-law duties.[16] Physical exhaustion does not automatically establish a downloadable resale right. |

5. **The proposed EOL-triggered unlock is technically possible only for a subset of games.** A signed final offline build and durable offline entitlement are feasible for self-contained games if planned early. They are not an “ISO” in the PC sense and cannot reconstruct authoritative multiplayer servers, economies, licensed cloud assets, user data or live operations. Hardware binding improves piracy control but is poor preservation: storage, secure elements and consoles fail. A universal automatic conversion also risks exposing keys, enabling pre-EOL piracy through clock/firmware attacks, and breaching third-party licences.

6. **The trigger should be title/service-specific, not “platform EOL.”** Use objective events: permanent delisting plus end of redownload; shutdown of required authentication/gameplay servers; or announced cessation of the platform entitlement service. Require advance notice and an outcome selected from a pre-contracted menu. This addresses games that die early and avoids waiting for an undefined platform death.

7. **Rights tiers can improve transparency but a five-tier ownership menu is commercially and cognitively heavy.** Transferability, offline access, support period and subscription status are meaningful product attributes. Yet “permanent,” “lifetime” and “revocable personal licence” can mislead; many customers will choose the cheapest tier without understanding future loss; regional SKUs fragment multiplayer/support; and transferable perfect digital copies can cannibalize new sales unless supply, one-active-copy enforcement and publisher participation are controlled. Charging at EOL to recover playability is especially weak: customers may perceive a ransom for continued use, rights may already have expired, and late uptake will not finance engineering reliably.

8. **Preservation needs institutional custody as well as consumer copies.** Consumer-held encrypted packages improve redundancy but do not preserve source, build tools, server code, metadata, patches, manuals or legal provenance. The Video Game History Foundation's 2023 US sample found only 13% of pre-2010 games commercially available (1,500-title sample, +/-2.5% at 95% confidence); this is evidence of access risk, not proof that 87% are physically lost.[17] EU law permits cultural-heritage institutions to copy works permanently in their collections for preservation, subject to lawful access, but public access remains separately constrained.[18]

## Stakeholder analysis

| Stakeholder | Incentive / benefit | Main risk or objection | Workable compromise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Players and advocates | Durable access, clear value, gifting/resale, competition | Account loss, shutdown, bandwidth/storage cost, confusing low-rights tiers | Standard rights label; minimum offline guarantee for self-contained purchases; notice and proportionate remedy |
| SIE / platform | Higher digital margin, security, unified delivery, subscription/store engagement | Engineering/escrow cost, piracy, liability, retail/regulatory backlash | Certification-based EOL plans; signed offline entitlements; capped support obligations; audit rather than public source release |
| Publishers / studios | Launch revenue, IP control, live monetization, manageable support | Rights-chain clearance, server/IP exposure, legacy QA and small-studio burden | Prospective contracts, architecture templates, safe-harbor outcomes, fee/technical support scaled by title type |
| Retailers | Hardware discovery, gifting, price competition | Loss of used/new software revenue and customer traffic | Restore title-specific digital codes and wholesale competition; retailer commissions; physical collector runs before cutoff |
| Regulators | Accurate marketing, conformity, competition, cultural access | Disproportionate mandates, trade-secret/copyright and cross-border complexity | Enforce disclosures/remedies first; monitor pricing/retail access; co-regulatory EOL code with measurable compliance |
| Archives, libraries, museums | Authentic binaries, documentation and research access | Anti-circumvention, on-premises limits, missing server/build dependencies | Accredited escrow deposits, preservation keys after trigger, metadata/build documentation, controlled researcher access |

Control and burden are asymmetric: SIE can mandate certification and operate entitlement infrastructure, while publishers must clear content and fund title-specific adaptation. Consumers and archives otherwise bear storage, obsolete hardware and legal uncertainty. A credible model must allocate duties at contracting/certification, when leverage and source materials still exist.

## Solution options

### Option 1: Digital-only status quo plus clearer disclosure

Require a checkout label stating: licence (not title ownership), transferability, offline capability, required accounts/servers, minimum announced service term, post-delisting redownload policy and shutdown remedy. Restore competitive retail sale of title-specific codes, rather than only wallet credit, and publish regional price histories.

- **Viability:** High technical/business feasibility and aligned with California disclosure law and the EU's planned code.[9][14]
- **Limits:** Transparency does not preserve games, guarantee access or replace resale. Retail codes add price competition but remain account licences.

### Option 2: Full rights-tier/lifecycle-pricing model from the input

Offer transferable, permanent-personal, limited/revocable and subscription licences at different prices, adding cheaper tiers as a title ages.

- **Viability:** Technically possible through a platform licence ledger; price discrimination may expand reach. Contractual transfer can be granted even where law does not require it.
- **Limits:** High publisher coordination, UX, fraud and regional-compliance cost; uncertain demand; cannibalization and arbitrage; no evidence supports the proposed $35-$70 spreads. “Revocable purchase” risks unfairness and disclosure liability. Lifecycle discounts already achieve much segmentation with less complexity. Do not make basic post-sale durability a premium upsell.

### Option 3: Universal platform-EOL offline unlock

At platform EOL, convert every account licence to a downloadable, hardware-bound/offline image.

- **Viability:** Medium for SIE-owned, self-contained titles; low catalog-wide. A platform can sign a final executable and offline entitlement.
- **Limits:** Undefined/late trigger, hardware mortality, unavailable third-party rights, server-dependent designs, huge final-build QA, security-key leakage and no guarantee users downloaded before infrastructure closure. It preserves consumer play better than production history.

### Option 4: Prospective title-level continuity standard and escrow

For games certified/recontracted from a future date, require a machine-readable rights label and an EOL plan selected at certification:

1. Signed offline patch/build with no periodic authentication for self-contained modes.
2. Local/private server package or documented protocol where safe and contractually cleared.
3. Continued redownload and offline entitlement validation for a stated minimum period.
4. If consumer release is infeasible, encrypted deposit of final binaries, patches, server components where available, documentation and keys with an accredited archive/escrow agent; release under controlled preservation terms at a defined trigger.
5. Advance shutdown notice, save/UGC export where applicable, and automatic pro-rata refund/store credit if the delivered life is shorter than the prominently disclosed term.

- **Viability:** Best balance. Prospective clearance avoids retroactive rights failures; a menu accommodates offline, multiplayer and live-service games; escrow protects secrets during commercial life.
- **Limits:** Nonzero certification and maintenance cost, archive funding, cross-border terms and possible “minimum term becomes maximum” behavior. Private servers cannot reproduce moderation, licensed services or viable populations.

## Recommended approach

Adopt **Option 4**, preceded immediately by Option 1 disclosures and retail competition. Do not adopt the proposed universal EOL ISO or full consumer rights-tier matrix as designed.

1. **Before January 2028:** SIE should publish scope by region/platform, define “digital at retail,” restore title-specific code competition, grandfather pre-cutoff discs, and display a standard five-field rights label before purchase.
2. **For new certifications/contracts:** require a title/service-specific continuity plan and rights clearance. Make durable offline use the default for purchased self-contained modes; keep subscription access explicitly temporary. Permit one optional transferable licence pilot, but do not build the policy around legally uncertain digital exhaustion.
3. **Trigger outcomes objectively:** required-server shutdown, entitlement/redownload cessation, or withdrawal before the disclosed minimum term, not an undefined console EOL. Give at least 12 months' notice where practicable, with shorter emergency/security exceptions and prompt remedies.
4. **Implement securely:** use SIE-signed final packages and offline entitlement receipts that can migrate to replacement compatible hardware, not one-console binding. Escrow decryption material separately; audit builds periodically so the final patch is not attempted after staff and dependencies disappear.
5. **Preserve comprehensively:** pair consumer continuity with funded deposits to qualified archives. Separate “playable customer copy” from preservation of source/build/server context and from public distribution rights.
6. **Price fairly through evidence, not a formula:** disclose rights and support term, allow retailer price competition, maintain accessible refund remedies, and test price/demand. Compare net prices including VAT/sales tax and sale frequency. Do not promise arbitrary tier discounts until willingness-to-pay, cannibalization and cost studies exist.

This approach aligns incentives better than a late unlock: engineering and clearance occur while a game is funded; SIE gains a trust differentiator before discs end; publishers retain active-life control; customers receive a defined minimum outcome; and archives receive legally documented materials. A voluntary code may be the fastest EU route after the Commission's June 2026 response, but measurable labels, triggers, audits and remedies are necessary to avoid symbolic compliance.[14]

## Risks and open questions

### Material risks and unintended consequences

- **Piracy/security:** Offline patches or server packages may expose keys, exploits, anti-cheat logic or personal data. Mitigate with title-specific threat review, clean-room server packages and delayed key release; do not promise release where safety cannot be managed.
- **Commercial distortion:** Transferable digital copies are perfect substitutes and may reduce primary sales; rights tiers may anchor customers to lower prices or make ordinary durability a luxury. A controlled pilot with one-active-entitlement transfer and publisher opt-in is safer.
- **Premature shutdown:** A disclosed minimum support term could become the routine shutdown date. Require actual notice, usage review and public EOL reporting, not only a contractual floor.
- **Exclusion:** Digital-only distribution burdens poor/slow-connectivity households, cash users, children/families, gift buyers and people needing accessibility support. Retail codes, preload services, resumable downloads and offline activation reduce but do not eliminate this.
- **Preservation theater:** Encrypted consumer packages without durable keys, compatible hardware or documentation can create the appearance of preservation while remaining unusable.
- **Small-publisher burden:** Uniform private-server or perpetual-support mandates could deter releases. Templates, SIE tooling and scaled requirements are preferable to exemptions that leave indie customers unprotected.
- **Rights expiry:** Music, likeness, middleware, brand and co-publishing agreements may prohibit a new offline/server distribution. Prospective standard clauses are critical; legacy titles need best-efforts triage rather than an impossible blanket promise.
- **Jurisdiction fragmentation:** US anti-circumvention and no general digital first sale, EU conformity/exhaustion complexity, and UK post-Brexit law make one global transferable product risky. A global contractual minimum with region-specific remedies is more realistic.

### Evidence gaps / questions requiring answers

- Does “at retailers in digital formats” mean title codes, wallet cards, download kiosks or another product, and will retailers set prices independently?
- Does the cutoff bind every third-party publisher and territory, and does it cover reprints, limited editions, imports and cross-generation releases?
- What are PlayStation's actual costs and failure rates for entitlement, redownload and CDN retention, and what portion of physical economics returns to SIE/publishers/retailers?
- What share of PlayStation purchases and players depend materially on discs, resale/lending, cash retail or constrained internet in the US, each EU state and UK? Unit download ratio alone is insufficient.
- Which current/future titles can pass an automated dependency audit as self-contained, authentication-dependent, multiplayer-server-dependent or live-service-only?
- Can SIE obtain prospective offline, migration, escrow and archival rights in all publisher/certification agreements by 2028, and who funds legacy remediation?
- What duration do consumers understand from “permanent,” “purchase” and “foreseeable future,” and what price premium, if any, will they pay for transfer/offline rights?
- Which accredited institutions can securely hold console keys, proprietary server code and personal-data-bearing assets, under which US/EU/UK access rules?
- How will account bans, death/inheritance, family accounts, sanctions, publisher insolvency and platform insolvency affect durable entitlements?
- What metrics will govern the EU EOL code expected to begin development by end-2026, and will noncompliance affect enforcement under Directive 2019/770?[14]
